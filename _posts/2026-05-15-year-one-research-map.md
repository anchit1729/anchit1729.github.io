As I get closer to the halfway point of my PhD, I feel myself thinking on two timelines at once. On the shorter timeline, I am trying to push out the first real research artifacts of my PhD: systems, studies, papers, and all the smaller pieces of work that have to become concrete before they can become anything else. On the longer timeline, I am trying to understand what these artifacts are supposed to add up to.

That second question has started to feel harder to postpone. It is not enough for the projects to be interesting on their own, or useful as training, or convenient because they are already nearby. I want the work to form a coherent research story: one that feels compelling and urgent to the community I am becoming part of, while still being honest about what I actually care about.

This has been on my mind more as my qualifying exams get closer, and with them the feeling that my research identity is becoming less hypothetical. Earlier in academic life, it can be easier to accept projects because they are available, or because someone more senior points you toward them, or because they are a way to get exposure and learn how a lab works. There is nothing wrong with that. A lot of research begins through proximity before it becomes personal. But at some point, especially in a PhD, the justification has to change. The question becomes not just "can I contribute to this?" but "is this part of the work I want to be known for thinking about?"

I am writing this because I am coming out of my first year with a clearer map of what I want to do, even if the territory itself still feels large and uneven. Not everything is settled, but the uncertainty has started to become more structured.

## Finding the shape of the work

The first year was partly about learning what questions I actually want to sit with for a long time. I came in with interests that were broad enough to be exciting, but also broad enough to be hard to act on. Over time, the pieces have started to arrange themselves into something more usable: haptics, multisensory interaction, dynamic data, uncertainty, and the ways people make sense of systems that are difficult to see all at once.

I think part of the difficulty is that research taste develops more slowly than research activity. It is possible to be busy before you know what kind of work you want to make. You can read papers, build prototypes, help with studies, and still not quite know what the center of gravity is. This year has been about trying to close that gap: to make the things I am doing line up better with the kind of researcher I am trying to become.

## Having a map

Having a map does not mean having a rigid plan. It means I can now see a few paths that are worth walking, and I can tell when a new idea belongs near the center of the work or is just interesting in passing. That distinction has become important. Research produces a lot of tempting side paths, and part of the job is learning which ones are worth following.

## Working on several projects at once

I am also learning what it feels like to work on multiple projects simultaneously. There is a version of this that is energizing: each project gives the others context, and switching between them can make the whole research direction feel more alive. There is also a version that is messy: progress becomes harder to measure, and it is easy to confuse being busy with actually moving the work forward.

One thing I had anticipated, but not fully understood, was how much the backlog of research submissions can shape the work that comes after it. Papers do not simply disappear when they are submitted, rejected, revised, or waiting somewhere in the review pipeline. They keep taking up space. They ask to be rethought, resubmitted, reframed, and occasionally defended to yourself again. I knew this would happen in an abstract sense, but I did not quite realize how much it would affect the early stages of new work.

This has felt especially true because I am also moving into a new research area. My earlier work came more directly from haptics, while the work I am trying to build now sits closer to information visualization. That shift is exciting, but it also means I am not just starting new projects; I am learning how a different community frames problems, evaluates contributions, and decides what counts as interesting. The backlog from one phase of work travels with me into the next one, which makes the transition feel less like a clean break and more like an overlap between research identities.

I also want this post to point toward some of the people, ideas, and papers that changed how I think about this period. Part of finding a research direction has been realizing that my own map is being drawn in conversation with other people's work: not just the papers I cite because they are relevant, but the ones that quietly adjust what I think the field is trying to do.

## Paris and the shape of collaboration

The internship in Paris became one of the clearest examples of this overlap between old and new work. It was exciting, but also a little nerve-wracking in the background for a long time. Before I actually arrived, there were about eight months of logistics happening in parallel with everything else: applying for external travel funding, which I eventually received from the Eurohaptics Society and which Inria also generously contributed to for flight costs, obtaining security clearance because I would be working with a government-affiliated research organization, and then getting the visa itself. By the time I finally went, the internship already felt like something that had been quietly running alongside my PhD for most of the year.

The project connected directly to my first PhD paper, which is currently under review after being rejected once, but it also stretched the idea behind that paper into a more situated context. Instead of thinking about haptics for data representation mostly through abstract or generic chart visualizations, the Paris project asked what haptics might mean in a setting where people are trying to maintain situational awareness and make decisions inside a control room.

That shift mattered. A chart can make a research idea clean enough to study, which is useful, but control rooms make the stakes and constraints harder to ignore. The data is not just there to be interpreted; it is tied to infrastructure, monitoring, uncertainty, coordination, and action. Working with a large wastewater treatment plant, one of the largest in the world, made the question feel much less hypothetical. What would future analytics systems for these kinds of rooms need to do? What kinds of information should stay peripheral, become tactile, or interrupt attention? What does "awareness" mean when the environment is already saturated with displays, alarms, routines, and expertise?

It was also my first external collaboration during the PhD. I worked with Dr. Arnaud Prouzeau from the ILDA group at Inria Paris-Saclay, and with Nicolas and Marine Rouit-Leduc from Meaningful Design Studio. That collaboration changed the texture of the work for me. It made the project feel less like an isolated extension of my own interests and more like something being shaped by different institutional, disciplinary, and design cultures. I also learned from Arnaud's experience working on collaborative visual analytics, which was useful for thinking beyond individual interaction with a visualization and toward situations where sensemaking is distributed across people, displays, and roles. I had to explain what I cared about in a way that made sense outside my immediate lab context, and I had to listen for what the project could become when other people brought their own ways of seeing to it.

Some parts of the research culture felt immediately familiar. People had a similar sense of what counted as foundational information visualization research, and there was a shared vocabulary around interaction, perception, data representation, and evaluation. But there were also differences in the kinds of work I was exposed to. The visualization labs at Inria have worked with large-scale collaborative displays, for example, which are not hardware platforms every lab, including mine at Waterloo, has readily available. Seeing that made me think more about how HCI research can accumulate exclusivity through access to particular infrastructures, settings, or partnerships.

That kind of exclusivity is not unique to HCI. It shows up in other parts of computer science too, especially in areas like machine learning or large-scale distributed systems, where the most cutting-edge work can depend on resources that only large organizations have. It exists in other fields as well, including mathematics, but in STEM it often becomes tied to money more directly, or to the byproducts of money: specialized equipment, institutional agreements, memoranda of understanding, and access to deployment contexts that are not equally available to everyone. I do not yet know exactly what to do with that observation, but it changed how I think about what kinds of research questions become easy or hard to ask.

The work-life balance also felt different from Canada, partly because the PhD structure itself is different. At Universite Paris-Saclay, the PhD program is shorter, closer to three years rather than four, and that changes the rhythm of graduate research. It made me more aware that the experience of doing a PhD is not a universal thing. Even when the papers, venues, and research problems overlap, the surrounding academic systems shape what the work feels like day to day.

I also enjoyed the collaboration because of how genuinely interdisciplinary it was. Computer scientists, designers, and control room operators were all contributing to the design process, and each group seemed to hold a different piece of the problem. That kind of setting made the project harder to reduce to a clean technical contribution, but also more interesting. It forced the question of what the system was actually for, and who needed it to make sense.

This is part of why I have started to think that travel during graduate school is valuable beyond conferences. Conferences are useful, obviously, but they can also compress travel into a familiar academic pattern: arrive, present, listen, network, leave. Spending longer somewhere else does something different. It exposes you to another research culture at a slower pace, where the differences show up not only in talks or papers but in meetings, design decisions, institutional assumptions, and casual conversations.

Other shorter trips reinforced this too. Visiting the Copenhagen HCI group and meeting [Tor-Salve Dalsgaard](https://torsalve.xyz/) again was a reminder of how small academia can feel, in a nice way. We had last met when he was in Waterloo three years earlier, and being able to catch up in a different research context made the field feel less like a set of disconnected institutions and more like an overlapping set of people who keep reappearing in each other's intellectual lives.

It also makes the PhD feel less sealed off from the rest of life. After nearly four years of living in Waterloo, the change of scenery was useful in a way that was not only recreational. Paris is an unusually dense cultural place to spend time in, and living there for a while made it possible to be immersed in it rather than simply pass through it. I was speaking in French, living around French routines, working in a French research environment, and learning more about French art and history in the gaps between work.

That might sound separate from research, but I do not think it is. Research taste is shaped by more than papers. It is also shaped by the kinds of systems, objects, histories, and public life you spend time around. Being in Paris meant being around different art, architecture, food, public spaces, museums, streets, and ways of moving through a city. For me, the value of travel was not just that it gave me access to a project. It also gave me more material to think with, and a reminder that the sensibilities I bring to research are formed by the world outside the lab too.

## What has changed after year one

The biggest change is that I feel less like I am trying to prove that I belong in a research space, and more like I am beginning to understand how to contribute to it. That is a subtle shift, but a useful one. It makes the work feel less like a performance of productivity and more like an ongoing attempt to build judgment.

## What I want to do next

The next step is to turn this map into systems, studies, and writing that can hold up outside my own head. I want to build things that make the research questions sharper, not just prettier. If the work is about helping people understand complex information through richer interaction, then the systems need to make that promise concrete.
