By the time I was supposed to leave for France, I had developed a sense of reluctance that was unexpected. The visit took about eight months to arrange: an external travel funding application, support from the Eurohaptics Society and Inria for the flight, security clearance for working with a government-affiliated research organization, and finally the visa process. All of this had unfolded alongside the rest of my work. Enough time had passed that the initial excitement had begun to wear down, and I found myself weighing how much time had already gone into making the visit possible against how little time I would actually have to work there.

Research visits are easy to describe in retrospect as exciting opportunities: a project comes together, the paperwork gets done, and eventually you arrive. From inside the process, it's full of uncertainties, all directly tied to your research output. Which projects would I be able to work on? What directions would my thesis take, depending on the outcomes of this visit? By the time I left, France was less somewhere I wanted to go and increasingly a commitment that had been sitting in the background of my life for most of a year.

Once I actually got there, however, things turned out quite positively. I spent time working with researchers and designers on questions about haptics, visualization, and situational awareness, and also made room for touristy things, taking in the sights and walking along the Seine anytime I stopped by Saint-Michel-Notre-Dame. The research did not take away from the rest of my time, and conversations in the lab, the museums and spaces I spent time in, and the rhythms of everyday life in France all became part of how I went about my day and thought through ideas for my PhD.

I was visiting Inria Paris-Saclay for my first external research collaboration during the PhD. The project grew out of my work on using haptics to represent data that would typically be hidden or rendered visually. It took my initial study on details-on-demand haptic line charts and situated it within the context of supporting awareness and decision-making in the control room of a large wastewater-treatment plant. I worked with Arnaud Prouzeau and the ILDA group at Inria, alongside Nicolas and Marine Rouit-Leduc of Meaningful Design Studio. That combination of computer science, design, and domain expertise made the project difficult to reduce to a tidy technical problem—which was precisely what made it valuable.

This post is mainly about me ranting about the things that became visible spending time in another country during my PhD. My stay in France changed how I thought about HCI, academia, the relationship between research and institutional resources, and the ways that life in the HCI-world connects to the real world. It also reminded me that academic travel offers something different from the compressed routine of attending a conference. Living and working somewhere, even temporarily, gives the place and its people time to affect the way you think.

## From charts to control rooms

The project connected directly to my first PhD paper, which was under review after a second round CHI rejection when I began planning the visit. As I mentioned earlier, it also took the idea behind that paper into a more situated context. Instead of thinking about haptics for data representation mostly through abstract or generic chart visualizations, the Paris wastewater project asked what haptics might mean in a setting where people are trying to maintain situational awareness and make decisions inside a control room.

Sure, abstract interfaces help conduct lab-controlled studies, which are useful, but the noise, stakes and confounds of a control room demands a different approach towards both system design and evaluation. The data is tied to physical infrastructure, uncertainty, and coordination between control-room and onsite operators. Working with a large wastewater-treatment plant made the question feel much less hypothetical. What would future analytics systems for these kinds of rooms need to do? What kinds of information should stay peripheral, become tactile, or interrupt attention? How is "awareness" supported when an environment is saturated with displays, alarms, and heterogeneous work routines? And, different from other types of control rooms in power generation facilities or energy distribution grids, how does wastewater present unique challenges? (Hint: temperature values, lag due to the speed of biological processes, and the physical speed of flowing water are all relevant factors)

## A different research culture

Some parts of the research culture at Inria felt immediately familiar. People had a similar sense of what counted as foundational information-visualization research, and there was a shared vocabulary around interaction, perception, data representation, and evaluation. They'd read the same famous books and knew of the same famous names (a few are themselves famous names across ILDA, AVIZ, and EX-Situ). But there were also differences in the kinds of work I encountered. The visualization groups at Inria have worked with large-scale collaborative displays, for example, which are not hardware platforms every lab--including mine at Waterloo--has readily available. Seeing that made me think more about how HCI research can accumulate exclusivity through access to particular infrastructures, settings, and partnerships. I've heard of similar practices even from colleagues in theoretical fields, where access to research areas can be gatekept by existing established names, thus also contributing to the perpetual concentration of power and influence at some academic institutions over others.

The work–life balance also felt different from Canada, partly because the PhD structure itself is different. In Europe, PhD programs are shorter, closer to three years rather than four, and that changes the rhythm of graduate research. It made me more aware of how different the experience of doing a PhD can be, and how much of that cultural context is lost by the time a paper is published. Even when the papers, venues, and research problems overlap, the surrounding academic systems shape what the work feels like day to day, and eventually the outputs that are produced. An international student on a three-year contract has a very different sense of urgency about their research and broader vision as compared to one with a four- or six-year window. At the same time, the stricter work/life split of the European PhD seems to help preserve habits that a longer program might not prompt students to pick up at all, such as knowing when not to keep going or being more open to (yourself or other people) prioritizing personal life over work when needed.

Spending time in France also made me think about how differently the status of a graduate student is understood across countries. The position occupied by students from engineering schools felt different from what I was familiar with in Canada, and in some ways closer to what I had seen in India and Hong Kong. These differences are not only about program length or working hours. They also concern social standing, institutional prestige, financial support, and the kinds of futures that graduate education appears to enable.

That comparison also made me think about the very different graduate-school experiences that exist within my own family. My grandfather completed a D.Litt. in Hindi literature in India, but the opportunities available to him were shaped by the socioeconomic conditions of the country during the 1970s and 1980s, as well as by the difference in funding and institutional support between the arts and STEM. Often, obtaining funding for travel and getting access to manuscripts was a challenge for him. In contrast, my own ability to travel internationally for research in computer science rests on opportunities and forms of privilege that have accumulated over time (for one, I'm pursuing grad studies in Canada), and yet, I still find myself stressing out about my situation at times.

## Travel beyond conferences

This is part of why I have started to think that travel during graduate school is valuable beyond conferences. Conferences are useful, obviously, but they can also compress travel into a pattern that starts to look the same: arrive, present, listen, 'network', leave. Spending longer somewhere else does something different. It exposes you to another culture at a slower pace, where the highlights end up coming not only from talks or papers but from casual conversations and day-to-day life. In my case, Paris was an unusually dense cultural place to spend time in (for various reasons, both good and bad), and living there for a while made it possible to be immersed in it rather than simply pass through it.

That might sound detached from research, but I do not think it is. Research taste is shaped by more than papers. It is also shaped by the kinds of systems, objects, histories, and public life you spend time around. Being in Paris meant being around different art, architecture, food, public spaces, museums, streets, and ways of moving through a city. Conveniently for me, there is also quite a bit of conceptual and application overlap between HCI/visualization/haptics and urban planning/design/art/museums. So to me, the value of the visit was in both the fact that it gave me more material to think with, and reminded me in a more material way how research is influenced by the world outside the lab in various ways that often do not translate to the camera-ready drafts that get published.
